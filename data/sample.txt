Title: Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Academic Question Answering

Abstract:
Recent advances in Artificial Intelligence (AI) have significantly transformed
academic research workflows. Researchers are increasingly relying on large language
models (LLMs) to summarize literature, extract insights, and generate hypotheses.
However, LLMs are prone to hallucination and may produce factually incorrect statements.
To address this limitation, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has emerged as a
powerful framework that combines information retrieval with generative models.

1. Introduction
Artificial Intelligence has become a fundamental tool in modern research.
From natural language processing to computer vision, AI models assist scholars
in processing vast amounts of information. In particular, language models such as
transformer-based architectures have demonstrated strong performance in question
answering, summarization, and reasoning tasks.

Despite these achievements, traditional language models rely solely on parametric
knowledge stored in their weights. This means they cannot access new or updated
information after training. As a result, they may generate outdated or incorrect responses.

2. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)
Retrieval-Augmented Generation addresses this problem by integrating a retrieval
component into the generation pipeline. Instead of answering questions purely from
internal knowledge, the system retrieves relevant documents from an external corpus.

The RAG pipeline typically consists of the following steps:
1. Document ingestion and chunking
2. Embedding generation
3. Vector database indexing
4. Query embedding
5. Similarity search
6. Context construction
7. Answer generation using an LLM

By retrieving relevant context, the language model produces grounded and
factually consistent answers.

3. Vector Embeddings
Vector embeddings are dense numerical representations of text.
They capture semantic meaning in high-dimensional space.
Two semantically similar sentences will have embeddings that are
close to each other according to distance metrics such as Euclidean distance
or cosine similarity.

Common embedding models include Sentence-BERT and other transformer-based encoders.
In practice, embeddings often have dimensions such as 384, 512, or 768.

4. FAISS and Vector Databases
Facebook AI Similarity Search (FAISS) is a library designed for efficient
similarity search over large vector collections. It supports multiple indexing
strategies, including exact search and approximate nearest neighbor search.

FAISS allows fast retrieval even when handling millions of vectors.
This makes it suitable for building scalable RAG systems.

5. Applications in Academic Research
RAG systems can function as academic assistants.
They enable users to upload research papers and ask domain-specific questions.
The system retrieves relevant sections and generates precise answers.

Such systems are especially useful in:
- Literature review
- Policy analysis
- Legal document inspection
- Scientific hypothesis generation

6. Limitations and Challenges
Although RAG improves factual grounding, challenges remain.
Chunk size selection affects retrieval performance.
Overlapping chunks help maintain context continuity.
Embedding model quality directly influences retrieval accuracy.

Additionally, the quality of generated answers depends on
the alignment between retrieved context and user intent.

7. Conclusion
Retrieval-Augmented Generation represents a promising direction
for building trustworthy AI systems in academia.
By combining retrieval mechanisms with powerful generative models,
researchers can construct intelligent systems that are both accurate
and context-aware.
